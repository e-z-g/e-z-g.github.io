The pipeline, and what’s honestly possible

Read this once before blaming the tool

Forcing the Spatial Scene toggle:
  1. Run the image through panel 1 and download the JPEG.
  2. Get it into the iPhone photo library as a photo: AirDrop it, or save to Files and share → Save Image. It should land in the main library, not in Screenshots.
  3. Open it in Photos on iOS 26+ and tap the spatial (hexagon) button top-right. The depth is inferred on-device, on the spot.
Toggle still not showing? The gate is not only metadata. Known additional requirements, all reported in the wild: the phone must be on Wi-Fi (mobile data silently fails), Reduce Motion must be off (Settings → Accessibility → Motion), the full-resolution original must be on the device (with iCloud-optimized storage, open the photo and start-then-cancel an edit to force the download), and it takes an iPhone 12 or newer on iOS 26. Photos may also need a little time after import before the option appears. Work through that list before blaming the EXIF.

Getting depth out: the Spatial Scene depth map itself never leaves the phone — Photos renders it live and exports no file containing it. What is extractable, and what panel 2 reads: Portrait-mode shots (HEIC aux disparity image, or the extra JPEGs appended to a shared Portrait JPEG), HDR gain maps, and segmentation mattes. Export the unmodified original from Photos, or AirDrop to a Mac — “most compatible” conversions usually strip the aux images.

Output format: 16-bit grayscale PNG, values scaled ×257 from the 8-bit source, plus a JSON sidecar naming the source urn and whether bright means near (disparity) or far (depth). That pair drops straight into MiDaS-style depth pipelines.

Recovering iOS's own depth — the screenshot trick: the Spatial Scene depth never leaves the phone as a file, but the screen renders it. Turn the effect on, tilt the phone to one extreme and screenshot, tilt to the other extreme and screenshot again: those are two views rendered from Apple's own depth map, and the pair mode above inverts the parallax back into that depth. Crop both screenshots identically to just the photo (UI chrome sits at zero parallax and wastes the range), tilt horizontally, and if the result looks inside-out the tilt went the other way — tick invert. A screen recording while tilting works too: load it as a video, scrub, and tag two frames as A and B. What you get is a reconstruction of the depth iOS rendered — relative, not metric, and carrying the renderer's own edge-stretching — but it needs no file-format tricks at all.

Spatial / Facebook & Meta video: Meta's side takes stereo video as Apple MV-HEVC (uploaded through the Meta Quest app; a Facebook feed post just shows it flat) or as plain full side-by-side video. Drop a .MOV/.MP4 above and the tool reads the stereo boxes — Apple's vexu (eyes, baseline, disparity adjustment) and the st3d SBS/TB tag — even when the browser can't play the codec. MV-HEVC's second eye is beyond every browser decoder, so flatten to SBS first (ffmpeg 7.1+ decodes MV-HEVC, or Spatialify on iOS) and feed the SBS back in: then you can grab a frame, split the eyes, and compute a real disparity map by block matching, exported in the same 16-bit PNG + JSON format. Facebook's own “3D photo” upload path (2D + depth pair) is long past its heyday — Stories dropped it and it is not something to build on now — so panel 3 goes the other way and hands Meta what it reliably eats: video.

Panel 3 — preview, and the hold: load a picture (plus a depth map, if you have one) to see the parallax rendered live, then record it. Flat hold needs no depth at all: it is your still, held for N seconds, as an ordinary video — the thing that uploads anywhere, and what Quest's own photo-to-3D conversion can then chew on. Wiggle oscillates the camera, which is what actually reads as three-dimensional to a phone or desktop feed, since a flat viewer never sees a second eye. Side-by-side writes both eyes into one frame for Quest and any SBS player. MP4/H.264 is requested first because Meta ingests it most happily; if your browser only offers WebM the tool tells you, and ffmpeg -i in.webm -c:v libx264 -pix_fmt yuv420p out.mp4 finishes the job. No depth map to hand? Pull one from a Portrait shot above, from a stereo video above, or from any monocular depth model — the 16-bit PNG this tool exports is the same format they emit.

HEIC decoding uses WebCodecs HEVC — works in Safari and in Chrome on hardware with an HEVC decoder. Where it can’t decode, the tool still lists the file’s contents and hands you the raw HEVC payload for heif-convert --with-aux or ffmpeg. Everything runs locally; nothing is uploaded.
