why

Sizes and the opcode grammar follow delv/graphics.py. Sized resources carry a four-byte header giving their own dimensions; every other class has a canonical size the format does not record.

Decode

Opcode classes

why

Which opcode wrote each pixel, for reference — see the Opcodes view. It used to drive the filter as well: long runs and distant copies were treated as deliberate structure and locked. That was wrong. The dither itself is what compresses into long copies, so it locked the noise and not the drawing, and measured worse on every resource class. Removed.

Indices E0–FB cycle at runtime (flame, water), so their exact values are load-bearing and stay protected.

Find the dither

why

Dither disagrees with its neighbours in every direction. A drawn line agrees along its own direction, so it survives. Diagonal protection is off by default: Cythera's dither is irregular rather than a clean checkerboard, and enough of it happens to line up diagonally that protecting diagonals mostly protects the dither — turning it off cleans half again as much for under a point of lost detail. Switch it on for artwork with real diagonal linework.

Flat patches only bites at pattern scale 2 and above. There the averaging window reaches past a uniform block and would drag its colour about; this pins any pixel whose eight neighbours are already identical. At scale 1 the window never reaches that far, so it changes nothing.

Sensitivity is the blunt dial: it goes to 1.00, but on these portraits detail starts breaking up above about 0.35 — Magpie's pupils go at 0.40. Reach for edge threshold first, which cleans nearly as hard without touching them.

Stray colours

why

Some resources carry isolated pixels in a wildly wrong hue — Erechtheus (0x881A) has 69 magenta ones across his face, quantisation accidents rather than paint. The eye finds an out-of-gamut dot instantly, so they are the most offensive thing left in the output.

The second slider keeps it out of drawn artwork. A stray reads as incoherent wherever it sits, so its own detector value cannot separate a magenta accident on a cheek from an odd pixel in a frame — both score about 1. What separates them is the company they keep: averaged over a neighbourhood, faces run 0.67–0.89 and drawn frames 0.24–0.59. Lower it to reach into linework, raise it to stay strictly inside photographic areas.

This flags a pixel whose chroma is unlike anything beside it, which is what separates an accident from a feature. A pupil is extreme in brightness but neutral in chroma, like the socket around it; a stray points somewhere in colour space that nothing near it points. On Erechtheus the magenta pixels sit 40–160 from their neighbourhood's median chroma and the pupils of Pelagon and Magpie sit at 0–15 — no overlap. At the default it removes five magenta pixels in six and leaves every pupil untouched. A pixel with a neighbour of its own hue is spared, so a gem or a coloured highlight survives. Set to 0 to disable.

Remove specks

why

For lone wrong-colour pixels that are damage, not dither. Only pixels with no similar neighbour are replaced; everything else passes through untouched. Both are off by default.

Lone literal pixels is the sharper version of the same idea, and needs a decoded resource: it cleans single pixels the encoder had to store as literals — the isolated red dots on the opcode map — at a fixed modest threshold, whatever the slider says. It is off because it is not free: a pupil one pixel across is also a lone literal, which is exactly what Pelagon has two of.

Denoise photo

why

Non-local means, for photographic content rather than dither. Averages pixels whose surrounding patch matches, so repeated texture reinforces and noise averages out. Leaves flat pixel art nearly untouched. Off by default.

Rebuild the tone

why

Raise the threshold to merge colours freely. Lower it to keep outlines hard.

The two smoothers reach the same place by different routes and measure within a whisker of each other. The bilateral weights each neighbour by how similar it looks; the guided filter fits a local linear model instead, which costs the same at any window size where the bilateral grows with it — at pattern scale 4 it is 156 ms against 252 ms. At scale 1, the default, the bilateral is slightly ahead. The guided filter also cannot produce the bright rim a bilateral sometimes leaves beside a strong edge.

Upscaling to 2× and resampling straight back is on by default, which sounds pointless and is not. You end up the size you started, but the interpolation invents samples along every edge and averaging them yields colours that were never in the palette — a two-tone dithered ramp comes back as an actual gradient. It took Magpie from 973 output colours to 1,524 and is the single biggest gain here. Untick it for hard native pixels; set upscale to 1× for neither.

Save

why

Going back to 256 colours cannot be a pure win: the artwork was dithered because the palette could not express these tones, so the error has to reappear as either banding or speckle. Nearest colour mostly recreates the speckle. Ramp-aware holds hue still across a region and steps only in lightness, which measurably cleans some resources — Pelagon's isolated colour outliers fall from 165 to 77 — and barely touches others.

Fewer competing ramps is cleaner and drifts further from the reconstruction; more is more faithful and noisier. A face lit warm on one side and cool on the other needs at least two, or a seam appears where the choice flips.
